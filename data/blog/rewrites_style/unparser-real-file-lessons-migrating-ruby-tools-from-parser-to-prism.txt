Every Ruby linter, formatter, and IDE you rely on is quietly betting its future on a parser swap, and that migration is harder than the slides admit.

A parser is the program that reads your source code as plain text and turns it into a structured tree that the rest of your tooling can reason about. That tree is called an abstract syntax tree, or AST, and it is a nested representation of the program: an expression such as a method call becomes a node with children for the receiver, the method name, and the arguments. Prism is the new parser that ships built into Ruby 3.4. It was written to be faster, more portable, and stable enough to serve as a shared foundation for the next generation of Ruby tooling. Until now, most of that tooling depended on the Parser gem instead. The Parser gem is a pure-Ruby parser that has been the de facto standard for over a decade, and it produces its own distinctive AST shape. An enormous amount of existing code, including RuboCop and the tools built around it, is written directly against that shape.

Unparser is one of those tools, and it makes a sharp test case for any migration. Unparser takes a Parser-style AST and turns it back into runnable Ruby source code. A tool that both reads and regenerates code will expose every place where the two parsers disagree about structure, so it surfaces problems that a read-only tool would quietly skip. That disagreement is where the real difficulty lives. Migrating from Parser to Prism is doable, but it is not a search-and-replace, because the two systems differ in their public API, in the exact shape and naming of the nodes they emit, and in how they treat the strangest corners of Ruby's grammar. Ruby's syntax is unusually expressive, so a parser has to handle constructs that are legal yet bizarre: chained range operators, flip-flop expressions that are missing one side, and rescue clauses that assign the caught exception into a constant, a method call, or even a zero-arity indexed assignment. Each of those is valid Ruby that a real parser must accept, and we had to patch Prism's Parser-translation layer to support some of them. This post walks through what that transition looks like in practice, where the AST shapes diverge, and why thorough testing is the step you cannot skip.
