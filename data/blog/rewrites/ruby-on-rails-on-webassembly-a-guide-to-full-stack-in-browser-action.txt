This guide explains how to run a full-stack Ruby on Rails application entirely inside a web browser by compiling it to WebAssembly, with no server or cloud involved. WebAssembly, often shortened to Wasm, is a portable binary instruction format that was originally introduced to bring high-performance and secure features to the web, and it now serves as one of the building blocks for cloud computing and embedded systems as well. Ruby on Rails, in turn, is a web framework focused on developer productivity and on shipping working software quickly, and it is the technology behind industry leaders such as GitHub and Shopify. By allowing a server-side framework like Rails to run locally in the browser, WebAssembly blurs the boundary between the frontend and the backend of a typical web application and opens up new architectural possibilities worth exploring.

The core idea this post demonstrates is that you can take an ordinary Rails application, the kind you would normally deploy to a server, and embed it directly into the browser so that both its frontend and its backend execute on the user's machine. This is a deliberately non-canonical use of the WebAssembly runtime, because developers often repurpose tools far beyond their originally intended design, much as the meaning of a work of art can diverge from its creator's intent depending on the audience. To make the demonstration concrete and familiar, the example recreates Ruby on Rails' famous "blog in 15 minutes," the tutorial that David Heinemeier Hansson (known as DHH) first showed off in a 2005 video, except this time the finished blog runs right in your browser rather than on a development server.

Over the course of the article, you will learn how to bring a Rails application into the browser in roughly fifteen minutes, what happens behind the scenes during the process of compiling, or "wasmifying," Rails, and where the future of Rails and WebAssembly may be heading. The journey begins by building a basic Rails application in the usual way on the command line, scaffolding a simple blog with posts that have a title, a date, and a body, and then packaging that application for WebAssembly so it can be loaded and executed entirely within the browser environment.
