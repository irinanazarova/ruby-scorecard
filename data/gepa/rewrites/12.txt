This report explains what early-stage venture funding looked like for developer-focused startups between January 2025 and March 2026, and what the companies that raised money actually did to earn it. To understand it, a few terms help. Developer tools, often shortened to devtools, are products built for software engineers rather than for general consumers: code editors, deployment platforms, testing services, and similar infrastructure. A funding round is an event in which a startup sells ownership stakes to investors in exchange for cash, and rounds are named by stage. The earliest is pre-seed, followed by seed, then Series A and Series B, each typically larger than the last as a company proves more of its business. Venture capital firms, abbreviated VCs, are the investment funds that supply much of this money. The analysis covers 1,140 such rounds across developer tools, cybersecurity, and infrastructure, drawn from a raw Crunchbase export of 1,206 deals after removing roughly 60 companies that do not sell to developers.

The piece sets out to answer two practical questions. The first is descriptive: how much money flowed, into which categories, and at what typical size per stage. Over the fifteen-month window these rounds totaled about $13.5 billion. Roughly half of the deals involved artificial intelligence, about half involved cybersecurity, and a fifth touched both, while the companies tied to neither still grew under the broader influence of AI. At every stage beyond pre-seed, AI companies tended to raise larger rounds than their peers.

The second question is explanatory: rather than stopping at the numbers, the report studies how about 130 funded companies actually grew, reconstructing their paths from founder interviews, podcast appearances, and press coverage to identify the specific product decisions and go-to-market tactics that drew investment. It also maps the most active investors by category, naming individual partners, so a founder knows whom to approach for a given kind of product. One caveat frames everything that follows: Crunchbase data is incomplete, since some rounds go unreported or are reported late, so the figures should be read as directional rather than exact.
